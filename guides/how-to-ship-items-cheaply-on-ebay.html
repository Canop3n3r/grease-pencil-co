How to Ship Items Cheaply on eBay

Shipping is where a lot of flips quietly lose money. A label that costs more than you guessed, a box that is a size too big, or a "free shipping" listing on a heavy item can wipe out the profit you worked out at the thrift store. The good news is that most shipping savings come from a few habits, not from secret tricks.

Postage rates and carrier rules change. This guide explains how the pieces work; always check the label price eBay shows you, and the carrier's current rules, before you list.

1. Weigh and measure every item, packed

The single biggest habit: weigh and measure the item as it will ship, in its box or mailer with padding, before you set shipping on the listing.

  • A shipping scale. A basic digital postal scale that reads ounces is enough. Kitchen scales work in a pinch but often top out too low for larger boxes.
  • A tape measure. Measure length, width and height of the outside of the box, and round up to the next whole inch.
  • Round up on weight, too. Lightweight packages are priced in small weight steps, so being one ounce over can push you into the next price tier. Guessing low means you pay the difference, and eBay may also adjust the label cost if the package doesn't match what you entered.

2. Dimensional weight basics

Carriers don't only charge by weight. For larger packages they also look at size, because a big light box takes up as much truck space as a heavy one. This is called dimensional (DIM) weight.

  1. Multiply length × width × height in inches to get cubic inches.
  2. Divide by the carrier's DIM divisor (USPS has used 166 for domestic packages; check the current number).
  3. The carrier charges whichever is higher: the actual weight or the DIM weight.

USPS generally only applies DIM weight to packages over one cubic foot (1,728 cubic inches), so most small and medium boxes are priced on actual weight. It matters most for big, light items like pillows, lampshades, stuffed animals and boot boxes.

Example (made-up numbers for illustration only): a 5 lb pair of boots ships in a 14 × 14 × 12 inch box. That is 2,352 cubic inches, which is over one cubic foot. 2,352 ÷ 166 = about 14.2, so the package could be charged as roughly 15 lb even though it weighs 5. Repacking into a 14 × 10 × 6 inch box (840 cubic inches) puts it back under the size threshold, so it is priced on its actual weight.

The fix is almost always the same: use the smallest box that safely fits the item.

3. Compare the main label options

eBay labels (bought through eBay's shipping label tool) are usually discounted compared to paying at the post office counter, and the cost comes out of your earnings, so the price you see is the price you pay. The common choices for small sellers:

  • USPS Ground Advantage. Usually the cheapest choice for light items, with tracking included. Good for most clothing, small parts, media and accessories. It is priced by weight and distance (zone), so a lighter, smaller package saves the most.
  • Priority Mail (weight-based). Faster, and priced by weight and zone like Ground Advantage. Sometimes close in price for mid-weight packages, so compare both on the label screen.
  • Priority Mail Flat Rate. One price for anything that fits in the flat rate envelope or box, regardless of weight (within the carrier's weight limit) or distance. Great for small, heavy items like tools, cast iron, auto parts and dense books. Usually a poor deal for light items, which ship cheaper by weight.
  • Padded or poly envelopes vs boxes. A poly mailer or padded envelope weighs less and costs less than a box, and it's fine for soft goods like clothing. Anything that can crush, bend or break belongs in a box.

Before you list, use eBay's shipping calculator or start a label with your packed weight and size to see what each service would cost to a far-away zip code. That's your worst case.

4. Free supplies (and the catch)

USPS gives away free Priority Mail and Priority Mail Express boxes, envelopes and flat rate packaging online and at post offices. The catch: they may only be used for Priority Mail or Priority Mail Express. Do not ship Ground Advantage in a free Priority box, even turned inside out.

For Ground Advantage, use your own boxes and mailers. Clean reused boxes from stores and deliveries work well; cover or remove old labels and barcodes. Buying poly mailers and bubble wrap in bulk lowers the cost per package, and many sellers reuse padding from their own deliveries.

5. Calculated vs flat shipping

  • Calculated shipping: you enter the packed weight and size, and eBay charges each buyer based on their location. Fair to buyers close to you, and it protects you on heavy items going far. It only works if your weight and size are accurate.
  • Flat shipping: one shipping price for every buyer. Simple, and fine for light items where costs don't vary much by distance. On heavy items, you lose money on far-away buyers unless you price for the worst case.
  • Free shipping: you pay the label, so the cost has to be inside your item price. Buyers like it, but only offer it when you've done the math.

6. Build shipping into your price

Whatever option you choose, the label, the box and the padding are real costs. Count them before you decide a flip is worth it, alongside your item cost and platform fees.

Example (made-up numbers for illustration only): you plan to sell a jacket with free shipping. Your far-zone label estimate is $9, and the mailer costs $0.50, so you set aside $9.50 for shipping. If sold comps say the jacket sells for about $30 shipped, you'd treat it as roughly $20.50 before fees and your cost, not $30. If that leaves too little profit, charge shipping separately or pass on the item.

Plug your own numbers into the free reseller profit calculator or the eBay fee calculator to see what you keep. Note that eBay generally charges its final value fee on the total the buyer pays, including shipping, which is covered in how to calculate real eBay profit after fees. For setting the item price itself, see how to price items using sold comps.

7. Packing fragile items

  • Wrap the item, then pad the box. Wrap it in bubble wrap or paper, then fill the space around it so it can't move. Shake test: if you hear it shift, add more padding.
  • Keep at least a couple of inches of cushioning between the item and every side of the box.
  • Box in a box for glass, ceramics and anything very fragile.
  • Protect the weak points: handles, spouts, corners, screens and lens glass.
  • Tape all seams with packing tape, not masking or duct tape.

Good packing adds a little weight, but it costs far less than a broken-item return. Take a quick photo of the packed item before sealing; along with your listing photos, it helps if there's ever a damage claim. If something does come back, see how to handle returns on eBay.

Common shipping mistakes

  • Guessing the weight instead of weighing it packed.
  • Using a box that's too big, which adds weight, filler and possibly DIM weight.
  • Flat rate for light items that would ship cheaper by weight.
  • Free shipping on heavy items without pricing in a far-zone label.
  • Ground Advantage in a free Priority box, which isn't allowed.
  • Forgetting supplies (mailers, tape, padding) when counting profit.
  • Buying postage at the counter when an eBay label is usually cheaper.
