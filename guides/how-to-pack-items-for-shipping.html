How to Pack Items for Shipping

Good packing does two jobs: the item arrives in the condition you described, and the package weighs and measures as little as possible so the label stays cheap. A broken item means a refund and a return, and an oversized box can eat the profit on a small flip. A simple, repeatable way to pack fixes both.

1. Match the package to the item

Soft, unbreakable items like clothing usually go in a poly mailer. Books and flat items fit padded or rigid mailers. Anything fragile, heavy or oddly shaped needs a box. Pick the smallest package the item fits in with room for padding, since size affects price as much as weight.

2. Pad fragile items on every side

Wrap the item, then fill the box so it cannot move when you shake it. Keep padding between the item and every wall of the box, including the top and bottom. Wrap separate pieces individually so they do not knock into each other. Reused bubble wrap, air pillows and packing paper all work.

3. Protect against water and scuffs

Put clothing, paper items and anything with a box of its own into a plastic bag before it goes in the mailer or box. Collectible boxes and books get an extra layer so the corners arrive clean.

4. Weigh and measure before you buy the label

Weigh the fully packed package, not just the item, and round up. Measure length, width and height of the outside. Entering the real numbers avoids extra charges later. A cheap digital shipping scale pays for itself quickly. See how to ship items cheaply on eBay.

5. Seal and label cleanly

Tape all seams on a box, not just the middle. Cover over or remove old labels and barcodes on reused boxes so the carrier scans the right one. Put the label flat on the largest side and keep tape off the barcode.

6. Keep supply costs low

Save boxes and padding from things you buy, and ask local stores if they have clean boxes they are throwing out. Buy mailers and tape in packs once you know which sizes you use most. Free carrier supplies usually only work for that carrier's specific service, so check the rules before you use them.

7. Count packing in your profit

Mailers, tape, labels and padding are real costs. Track roughly what each package costs to pack and include it when you work out profit. See how to calculate real profit after fees.

8. Photograph high-value packages

For anything expensive, take a quick photo of the item and the packed package before sealing it. It helps if a buyer says something arrived damaged or different. See how to handle returns on eBay.

Example (made-up numbers for illustration only):

You sell a ceramic mug for $24. You first grab a large box from your stash, but the packed weight and size would push the label price up. You switch to a small box, wrap the mug in two layers of bubble wrap, fill the gaps with paper so nothing moves, and weigh it at just over two pounds. Your supplies for this package cost about $1, which you log next to the sale so your profit number is real.

Common mistakes

  • Guessing the weight and getting charged more after the label is used.
  • Boxes that are too big for the item, which raises the shipping cost.
  • Loose items that rattle inside the box and break in transit.
  • Leaving old labels showing on reused boxes.
  • Forgetting packing costs when working out profit.
